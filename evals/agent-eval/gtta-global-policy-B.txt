### OPTIONS

Option A — Federated Architecture (recommended base posture).
Build separate EU-resident and US-core data planes; access China via licensed partner with separate product variant or API layer.
Pros: Satisfies GDPR/AI Act data localisation; preserves US speed; contains China exposure; scalable to additional jurisdictions.
Cons: Higher infrastructure cost; engineering complexity; requires three compliance stacks; partner model for China creates IP and control risk.
Trade-offs: Upfront capex vs. optionality; slows feature parity across regions.

Option B — EU-First Compliance Architecture.
Build to EU AI Act + GDPR standards as the global baseline; accept higher cost as the price of selling into regulated verticals globally.
Pros: Defensible with the most demanding enterprise buyers globally; positions for EU market leadership; hedges against US federal action; simplifies compliance stack.
Cons: Overbuilds for US market requirements today; slows product velocity; may be unnecessary for non-regulated B2B customers.
Trade-offs: Compliance premium vs. speed disadvantage in US and APAC markets.

Option C — US-Core, EU-Lite.
Optimise for US market; treat EU compliance as a bolt-on; defer China until commercially material.
Pros: Maximises near-term US velocity; lower compliance overhead; defensible while EU enforcement is nascent.
Cons: Creates technical debt that is expensive to unwind as EU enforcement matures; EU enterprise customers are already asking for AI Act compliance roadmaps; DPF collapse risk is unmitigated.
Trade-offs: Speed today vs. forced remediation cost at enforcement trigger.

Option D — Single-Jurisdiction Focus.
Exit or defer China; serve only US and EU; build to a single federated stack.
Pros: Eliminates the hardest compliance dimension (China); concentrates investment; simplifies architecture.
Cons: Foregoes China market optionality; may not be available if existing customer or investor commitments exist.
Trade-offs: Strategic simplicity vs. market size.
